# Magma inspiration from Into the Night

Designer research brief | 15 October 2026

## The brief in one page

Magma is a CDMX-based living archive for personal creative projects, unfinished work, observations, events, and conversations. Its longer-term home is a physical social club. The identity needs to connect these two lives: the things people make and keep, and the places where they meet and make something together.

The annotated pages from *Into the Night: Cabarets and Clubs in Modern Art* suggest a powerful precedent: a club can be an active part of artistic production, not simply a venue in which finished art is displayed. Conversation, performances, publications, interiors, invitations, objects, and shared jokes can all contribute to its identity.

For Magma, the most useful idea is a community that produces its own culture and leaves traces of it. The website can hold those traces without becoming a museum of a scene that has already ended. The physical club can bring them back into circulation through encounters and new work.

Nostalgia remains central, but it should make room for different people's memories. It need not mean a single decade, a uniformly distressed surface, or a historically reconstructed cabaret. A remembered song, an unfinished sketch, a photograph, a ticket, or a handwritten note can each carry a different relationship to time. The identity should give these things a shared home without making them look alike.

Working concept, developed for Magma rather than quoted from the book: **A living archive where different people's memories meet, and new ones are made.**

The emotional range is wider than quiet archival warmth. The references also contain absurdity, theatricality, bodily movement, sensuality, myth, and joyful strangeness. Maja's marginal notes, “Joyful Inferno” and “Horror and Ecstasy,” are particularly useful prompts for exploring that range. They are not yet proposed public taglines.

The designer's task is to explore how Magma can feel personal, culturally alive, and unmistakably rooted in CDMX, while accommodating many authors and disciplines. Café de Nadie and the Carpa Amaro are important local reference points. Chat Noir, Mbari, Fledermaus, the Italian cabarets, and the other cases expand the conversation; they should not displace its local centre.

An identity should work on a personal project page, a small printed invitation, an event programme, a collective publication, and eventually a physical room. These are different scales of the same community, not a requirement to launch every format now. The distinction from an event-listing brand is purpose: events are one way relationships and work begin, not the whole reason Magma exists.

## Gathering as a way of making art

The recurring subject is not a particular decorative style. It is a social arrangement: people from different disciplines sharing a place in which making, showing, arguing, performing, and spending time together can overlap. In the introduction, conviviality is not separate from serious artistic work. It is part of the conditions that make the work possible.

> These intimate and convivial settings gathered painters, sculptors, writers, poets, illustrators, musicians and dancers to create new work, debate current affairs, stage performances, organise exhibitions and produce publications that challenged the social and cultural norms of their time.

Q01 | Ostende, p. 12 | IMG_8858.

> Providing an intellectual and stimulating atmosphere for artists across all disciplines, their programmes of performances merged avant-garde experiments with popular entertainment.

Q02 | Ostende, p. 12 | IMG_8858.

> Salis orchestrated a creative and cross-disciplinary dialogue between painters, illustrators, musicians, poets and songwriters.

Q03 | Chat Noir overview, FO, p. 25 | IMG_8869.

> The Chat Noir’s spontaneous events programme ranged from poetry performances to improvised monologues, satirical songs and debates on contemporary politics.

Q04 | Chat Noir overview, FO, p. 25 | IMG_8869.

The highlighted Impressionists on p. 26 establish an earlier pattern of artists gathering in cafés. The following Chat Noir pages add the organisational work behind a scene: Émile Goudeau's literary group, its journals, Salis' programming and premises, and the contributions of artists and performers. The book does not describe creativity as the achievement of a logo or a room alone.

### For Magma

Make room for participation that is not a polished performance of professional identity. Someone can be a writer, a listener, a collaborator, and a beginner at different moments. Personal projects and works in progress should belong as naturally as announcements and finished pieces.

The brand should suggest a place to contribute and return to. Avoid implying that membership is only for an already recognised artistic elite. Equally, do not flatten the references into generic networking: their shared activity has content, disagreement, humour, and artistic stakes.

## The publication and the club sustain each other

The connection between gathering and publishing is the most direct historical precedent for Magma's digital and physical relationship. The journal carries work beyond an evening or an address; the club gives publishing a social life. The Mbari passage expresses this especially clearly.

> Each was an extension of the other in the intellectual context of post-independence Nigeria: the club materialised the intentions of the pages in a physical space, while the journal expanded the metaphorical borders of the club across Africa and beyond.

Q05 | Ostende, p. 14 | IMG_8860. “Each” refers to the club and journal in the preceding discussion of Mbari and *Black Orpheus*.

> Chat Noir in Paris and Schall und Rauch in Berlin issued their own journals, gathering together the work of illustrators, painters, writers, poets, journalists and songwriters.

Q06 | Ostende, p. 14 | IMG_8860. Excerpt beginning after “Cabarets such as the”.

> The Ibadan club comprised an open-air courtyard for holding literary and theatrical events as well as an art gallery and a library.

Q07 | Ostende, p. 14 | IMG_8860.

The marked Chat Noir passage also connects artistic vitality with economic continuity:

> With Salis’ entrepreneurial direction and the fresh talent of writers, artists and performers, the Chat Noir and its journal became popular and financial successes.

Q08 | Cate, p. 29 | IMG_8872.

The newspaper plate in IMG_8876 makes this relationship visible: a recognisable masthead, illustration, local symbolism, editorial columns, and publication information share one object. The Schall und Rauch cover, *Black Orpheus*, *Irradiador*, and the Mexican book covers show that this relationship can produce very different graphic languages.

### For Magma

Think of the digital archive as a place where the community keeps writing, not only a promotional channel for a future venue. A project can lead to a conversation; a conversation to an event; an event to images, writing, or a collaboration that returns to the archive. This is a proposed relationship between activities, not a new product specification.

The designer can explore a consistent editorial vocabulary across invitations, project entries, programmes, and collected material. Continuity should come from recognisable relationships and care, not from forcing every contribution into one house aesthetic.

## Nostalgia that leaves room for the future

The references are historical, but many of their subjects were trying to be insistently contemporary. This is a productive tension for Magma: affection for the traces of life can coexist with unfinished experiments and future desires.

> in artistic cabarets and clubs, potent references to older traditions commingled and merged with heightened aspirations for the future.

Q09 | Introduction continuation, printed page number outside the photograph | IMG_8866. Excerpt following “Indeed,”.

> The immersive atmosphere of the cabaret’s interior was conceived by Salis as an eclectic collage of time periods.

Q10 | Chat Noir overview, FO, p. 25 | IMG_8869.

> the diversity of art forms, the plurality of voices and the vulnerability of the artwork produced in these spaces, revealing an alternative and expansive view of art beyond the high modernist canon.

Q11 | Ostende, p. 13 | IMG_8859. Excerpt following “Into the Night celebrates”.

The highlighted introduction also stresses how ephemeral gestures and fragile paper objects can fall outside established art histories. The list on p. 12 includes drawings, sketches, plans, invitations, posters, scores, and programmes. Page 13 continues with the limited visibility of women working in dance and performance, and the elusive contributions of spectators, patrons, and amateurs.

The photographs themselves add a second layer of memory: these are not neutral scans. They show reading, selection, underlining, coloured marks, and handwritten reactions. That does not make every photographic detail a proposed brand asset, but it does show a real relationship with the material.

### For Magma

Treat nostalgia as personal significance, not an instruction to impersonate 1920s print. Some contributions may feel old; others should look unmistakably current. The shared value is that someone wanted to keep or share them.

Avoid an identity so complete and period-specific that it leaves no room for its members' own histories. The archive can hold imperfect, temporary, modest work without artificially ageing it. Its warmth should come from authorship and use, not only paper textures.

## A total environment across ordinary objects

The selected passages repeatedly dissolve the boundary between art and its setting. Interiors, performance, clothing, furniture, menus, printed matter, and small objects are not merely packaging around the “real” artwork. They participate in the experience.

> Clubs conceived as total environments stimulated a synthesis of the arts and collaborations between artists of different disciplines. From the pin badges worn by the waiters to the cocktail menus, the objects designed for the Cabaret Fledermaus articulated a philosophy for everyday living through the association of art, design and craft.

Q12 | Ostende, pp. 17-18 | IMG_8863 and IMG_8864. One continuous passage across the page turn.

> He strove to eliminate the hierarchical divisions between the various forms of artistic expression and attempted to expand the aesthetic concept from painting to fashion, furnishing, design, theatre, cinema and architecture in a decisive and articulated idea of ‘total art’.

Q13 | Futurism essay, p. 140 | IMG_8878. “He” refers to Giacomo Balla.

> The design was intended to complement an eclectic performance programme

Q14 | Cottrell, p. 87 | IMG_8891. Highlighted excerpt; the sentence continues with examples.

Fledermaus offers the scale of the everyday object; Balla's work extends the idea across disciplines and domestic life; the Cave of the Golden Calf explicitly connects interior design and programming. They suggest a relationship among things, rather than a requirement that everything be ornamented.

### For Magma

Test a design direction on both a screen and a physical object. A programme, small print, sign, or keepsake may reveal something a homepage cannot. The question is whether they feel related and capable of belonging to people's lives.

Do not confuse a coherent environment with visual uniformity. A flexible organising language could hold work by very different people. Nor should theatrical references make basic navigation, reading, or participation difficult.

## Imagined worlds and playful thresholds

The marked pages are drawn to spaces that feel apart from everyday life: basements, theatrical entrances, shadows, myths, moons, cats, devils, and stories. The underlying interest is not only a set of motifs. It is the experience of crossing into a world that people have made together.

> both sites materialised the desire to stage an immaterial world in physical space.

Q15 | Ostende, p. 18 | IMG_8864. Excerpt referring to Fledermaus and Chat Noir.

> Legends and fables were gathered, reported and circulated in these spaces, expressing a renewed fascination with, or commitment to, other worlds.

Q16 | Ostende, p. 18 | IMG_8864.

> utopian site somewhere between a church and an anti-museum where works outside the dominant traditions could find a home.

Q17 | Ostende, p. 18 | IMG_8864. Excerpt from a sentence qualified by “at their best”.

The long orange-marked description of the Cabaret del Diavolo is particularly important:

> The artist scrupulously followed the iconological programme of Gori, who in his theatrical fiction was Minos, the judge of the damned, accompanying the public on a kind of Dantean journey in reverse: from Paradiso they descended to Purgatorio, before finally arriving at the heart of the club, Inferno. This journey was interspersed with esoteric games and exuberant intervals before leading to the definitive place of sin, Inferno, for refreshment and conversation.

Q18 | Futurism essay, p. 144 | IMG_8881. The artist is Fortunato Depero; a handwritten heart sits beside this passage.

The destination is revealing: after the theatrical journey come refreshment and conversation. The fantasy serves a social encounter. Nearby descriptions connect the rooms to lighting, furniture, hearts, flames, devils, leaves, and puppet theatre. The red devil poster in IMG_8885 condenses that larger world into a portable invitation.

### For Magma

Explore a sense of arrival, discovery, and shared mythology. Small recurring symbols might acquire meaning through actual events and relationships. That is different from choosing an occult-looking emblem before the community has a story for it.

The invitation-only Diavolo model is a historical detail, not a recommendation to make Magma inaccessible. Intimacy and mystery need not mean social exclusion. A person can feel that they are entering a distinctive world while understanding how to participate.

## Movement and emotional intensity

The Fuller portrait, Balla's movement study, dancing figures, kinetic interiors, and musical references prevent this collection from becoming only an archive of static print. Bodies and movement are central. The material ranges from delicate, flowing lines to hard-edged geometry and exuberant silhouettes.

> music served as a constant source with which to harmonise the abstract rhythm of the decoration.

Q19 | Futurism essay, p. 143 | IMG_8880. Excerpt following “For Balla,”.

> reveals the temptation of a joyful inferno

Q20 | Charlotte Caillot, quoted in the Futurism essay, p. 142 | IMG_8879. Underlined excerpt within a longer quotation about the staircase.

> ‘Speed-lines’ with dynamic curves, ‘noise-lines’ with fragmented and angular segments, ‘trajectory-lines’ that express a vital flowing energy in sinuous curves

Q21 | Futurism essay, p. 142 | IMG_8879. Excerpt; the sentence continues with “force-lines” and the resulting coloured shapes.

> Strength, dynamism, playfulness, Italian style and originality.

Q22 | *Futurismo* article quoted in the Futurism essay, p. 143 | IMG_8880.

Maja's handwritten **“Joyful Inferno”**, accompanied by a flame on p. 142, is distinct from the printed phrase quoted above. **“Horror and Ecstasy”**, written under the Weimar overview on p. 213, echoes part of the nearby title *Dances of Vice, Horror and Ecstasy*. These annotations suggest attraction to emotional contrasts: pleasure and unease, warmth and danger, beauty and oddness. That is an interpretation of the selection, not a confirmed naming decision.

Loïe Fuller's image offers expansiveness and transformation rather than a literal costume brief. Balla's *Écarté. Study of Movement* offers a spare alternative to the densely decorated interiors: a few looping lines can imply a body and an action. The Depero-related textile offers rhythmic repetition and playful, constructed figures.

### For Magma

Let the identity have both quiet and animated registers. Personal writing and reflective work need calm; events and invitations may carry more theatrical energy. Movement can inform composition, spacing, line, or sequence without demanding constant screen animation.

“Joyful Inferno” is worth exploring as an internal mood phrase. It should not automatically turn Magma into a devil-themed nightclub or replace its wider commitment to personal creative life.

## CDMX and collective cultural identity

The Mexico pages give this research a local foundation. Café de Nadie appears as an actual meeting place, a subject for artworks and writing, and a symbol through which a group described itself. The Carpa Amaro extends the discussion to popular entertainment and accessible exhibition-making.

> developing a literary style and visual language rooted in the experience of contemporary urban life.

Q23 | Café de Nadie overview, LJ, p. 169 | IMG_8886. Excerpt describing Estridentismo.

> it was a place of literary and artistic experimentation and cultural hijinks.

Q24 | Klich, p. 170 | IMG_8887. Excerpt following a colon.

> Both real and metaphorical, the Café de Nadie embodied their unconventional art and collective action.

Q25 | Klich, p. 170 | IMG_8887.

> This tension between the international and the local, avant-garde and artisan, characterised Estridentista visual production

Q26 | Klich, p. 172 | IMG_8889. Excerpt; the sentence continues with Germán Cueto's masks.

> Kahlo’s work points to the sense of a continued need for strong visual symbols of collective action and the cultivation of alternative exhibition spaces.

Q27 | Klich, p. 172 | IMG_8889.

The highlighted narrative ties Estridentismo to post-revolutionary change and the experience of a modern city. The Tarde estridentista combines readings, music, and an exhibition. The café also supports less spectacular activity: conversation, reading, writing, and planning publications. Its public mythology and daily use are connected, but they are not identical.

The pages on Madrid's tertulias, international innovators, and local craft complicate a simple local-versus-foreign choice. Klich describes an international visual language mediated through local references and an earthy palette. The value for Magma is not a prescribed Mexican aesthetic; it is an identity made in a particular city while remaining open to exchange.

The source also describes the proposed ¡30–30! club as:

> a self-funded cooperative restaurant space based on the model of the ‘café-concert and cantina’ that could ‘create a cordial environment propitious for the aims’ of socialising, intellectual exchange and exhibition of artworks, independent of official restrictions.

Q28 | Klich, p. 172 | IMG_8889. Excerpt following the organisation's name. The next page states that the club never materialised as such; this is a proposed model, not a realised venue.

### For Magma

Begin with the cultural life of CDMX now, not a transplanted fantasy of Paris or Berlin. The useful historical connection is a place becoming part of a community's identity through what happens there.

A visual language can be locally grounded without relying on a stock collection of national symbols. Look at contemporary contributors' work, language, habits, and materials. Let the relationship between experimental form and everyday life guide the research.

Maja's handwritten **“Research”** on p. 169 brackets the roster of Estridentista participants. A focused next research list is Germán List Arzubide, Arqueles Vela, Salvador Gallardo, Xavier Icaza, Ramón Alva de la Canal, Jean Charlot, Fermín Revueltas, Leopoldo Méndez, Germán Cueto, Manuel M. Ponce, and Silvestre Revueltas. This is a reading list from the marked page, not evidence that all shared one visual style.

## Access and the politics of belonging

The collection values refuge, individual expression, and ways of reaching people outside conventional institutions. But these benefits were uneven. The strongest design lesson is to ask who can actually use and shape the space, rather than assuming an unconventional look makes it inclusive.

> Searching for intimacy and solitude in the anonymous crowd, the urbanite found refuge in the space of the café, bar or cabaret, where the transitory existence of modern city life could be negotiated.

Q29 | Ostende, page immediately preceding p. 17; number cropped | IMG_8862.

> It could also become a space that protected individual expression and – at times – allowed for non-normative sexualities and gender identities to be articulated.

Q30 | Ostende, same page | IMG_8862. The qualification “at times” is essential.

> privately organised cabaret events held in people’s homes were attended by black audiences of all classes who were denied access to segregated venues like the Cotton Club.

Q31 | Ostende, p. 17 | IMG_8863. Excerpt about Harlem rent parties following “These”.

> Cabarets offered spaces where lesbian, gay and queer artists could express themselves freely, and particular clubs such as Eldorado and Toppkeller were known for welcoming transgender performers and customers.

Q32 | Weimar overview, FO, p. 213 | IMG_8894.

> By co-opting this democratic and popular space for their own purposes, ¡30–30! used the model of the itinerant cabaret as a vehicle for their own socialist project and artistic expression.

Q33 | Café de Nadie overview, LJ, p. 169 | IMG_8886.

The Carpa Amaro material makes access concrete: prints chosen for reproducibility and affordability, popular performances, work pinned to tent walls, and an exhibition with no admission fee. The introduction's account of Ball and Hennings also connects cultural gathering with refuge from war. The subsequent Weimar passages show how vulnerable such spaces could be to suppression.

### For Magma

The relevant ambition is a welcoming place for individual expression, including people who do not feel at home in established creative institutions. Belonging should not depend on already having impressive credentials or knowing the right people.

This is not a request to erase curation or every boundary. It is a prompt to make those boundaries intentional, understandable, and compatible with the project's community-first direction. The design should communicate warmth and permission to participate, not only exclusivity and cultural authority.

## Humour and contradiction without romanticisation

Absurdity is a serious part of these references. The Incohérents, caricatures, theatrical devils, visual puns, and the anthropomorphic Café de Nadie make space for art that can also be funny. Yet the marked critiques insist that aesthetic rebellion and social liberation are not interchangeable.

> The Incohérents carried fumiste nonsensical, anti-bourgeois humour to the extreme, producing extraordinary works of art that foreshadowed the Dada, Surrealist and conceptual approaches of the twentieth century.

Q34 | Cate, p. 27 | IMG_8871.

> For instance, the satire and proto-Dada humour of the Chat Noir artists cohabited with conservative societal attitudes, sometimes expressed via militarist and nationalist views. The aristocratic visitors of the Cave of the Golden Calf and the elitist bohemia of the private club Rasht 29 in 1960s Tehran somewhat temper the romanticised legacy of their radical vision.

Q35 | Ostende, p. 19 | IMG_8865.

> In sum, the club’s iconography, designed as a sensorium for hedonistic pleasures, was fuelled by the imperial mentality of the period, in which land and people were conquered and other cultures were exoticised.

Q36 | Cottrell, p. 87 | IMG_8891.

> As the cabaret grew from an underground or avant-garde form to a genuinely popular entertainment, the heterogeneity of its audience compelled it to dilute its message.

Q37 | Laurence Senelick, quoted by Ostende, p. 19 | IMG_8865.

The highlighted Laura Winkiel passage on p. 19 critiques the aestheticisation of racialised spectacle. The London material addresses colonial imagery, while the Weimar overview examines the contradictions surrounding Josephine Baker and regimented performance. Cate's discussion of Louise France and Marie Krysinska records women's contributions alongside barriers to recognition. The Mexico essay also describes homophobic attacks by the Treinta-treintistas against their rivals in surrounding, unhighlighted text.

These are not marginal objections to otherwise uncomplicated models. They are part of what this selection is asking us to retain. The Eldorado photograph covered in Nazi imagery records repression and appropriation of a queer venue; it is not an aesthetic reference for Magma.

### For Magma

Keep humour, oddness, and freedom to experiment. Reject colonial fantasy, racial caricature, misogyny, homophobia, and prestige-based exclusion as ingredients of the identity. Avoid using historical terms such as “primitive” as celebratory design language.

The growth quotation is a historical warning, not proof that wider access necessarily destroys a community. A better question is how Magma can become more welcoming while preserving meaningful participation and a recognisable purpose.

## Visual references for the designer

These observations describe the supplied photographs. Photographed colours, lighting, and printed reproductions are not reliable specifications for original artworks or a final palette. Historical images are research references, not a cleared asset library for a new identity.

### Silhouettes and recurring characters

IMG_8868 shows a cat balanced on a crescent moon against a vivid red page. IMG_8874 shows the Chat Noir poster's cat, halo-like circle, irregular lettering, and black, yellow, and red hierarchy. IMG_8877 shows shadow-theatre silhouettes, including figures, a winged creature, and boats. IMG_8885 shows Depero's red devil with a trident and strongly organised display type.

Their shared quality is immediate recognition through shape. A character can move between sign, print, performance, and object. For Magma, this suggests exploring whether an original recurring symbol is useful. It does not imply adopting the Chat Noir cat or the Diavolo devil.

### Flowing bodies and economical lines

The red-tinted Fuller portrait in IMG_8867 turns fabric into an expansive form. IMG_8883, Balla's *Écarté. Study of Movement* (1920), does nearly the opposite: a small vocabulary of blue loops and strokes suggests action on a mostly open ground. Together they offer a wide range for motion, scale, and composition without dependence on literal dancing figures.

### A room full of voices

The Chat Noir scenes in IMG_8870 and IMG_8872, and Ramón Alva de la Canal's café image in IMG_8888, are dense with people, objects, words, and overlapping activity. They suggest collective authorship and multiple centres of attention. A possible translation is an editorial structure that lets different works coexist. Copying their density directly into every interface would sacrifice readability.

### Print as something to keep

IMG_8876 presents the Chat Noir journal; IMG_8889 the covers of Vela, Maples Arce, and List Arzubide; IMG_8890 the *Irradiador* cover. Mastheads, block lettering, illustration, columns, numbering, and limited colour create objects with a strong public voice. The membership card in IMG_8880 adds a more intimate scale: a small object that marks participation.

Explore the feel of a publication, invitation, or kept object without making every digital page imitate a newspaper. Typography and editorial framing can carry continuity while contributors retain their own voices.

### Ornament as a shared world

IMG_8875 shows Willette's *The Green Virgin*, with an elongated figure, patterned clothing, and a raised cat. IMG_8881 and IMG_8882 connect furniture, wall motifs, and social use. IMG_8884 shows brightly coloured, angular devils, flames, repeating figures, and a patterned border. The cats and moons above the text in IMG_8864 offer another form of playful repetition.

The useful question is how a motif might become part of a lived environment. The answer could be restrained or exuberant. It need not be a maximalist interior or an inherited set of sacred and occult symbols.

### The venue as a character

Fernando Bolaños Cacho's *El Café de Nadie* in IMG_8890 turns the building into a face-like form with an open mouth and objects moving out into the street. This is an especially apt image for a space whose cultural activity exceeds its walls. For Magma, it suggests personality and exchange between the archive, its people, and the city, rather than a literal instruction to put a face on the logo.

## Questions to take into the design process

1. How can the identity feel like somewhere people belong, rather than a service they consume?
2. How can it hold many personal nostalgias without assigning everyone the same retro style?
3. What should remain recognisable when a member's own work takes the foreground?
4. Where should Magma feel calm and intimate, and where can it become theatrical or mischievous?
5. What would make a small printed invitation worth keeping after the event?
6. How can CDMX be present through actual cultural life rather than generic local symbolism?
7. Does Magma need a character or emblem, or would a strong editorial language leave more room for its people?
8. Can the identity connect a personal project, a collective publication, and a physical room without promising a finished venue before one exists?

A useful first presentation could show two or three exploratory directions applied to the same small set of situations: a personal project, an invitation, a printed programme, and a physical sign or object. These are comparisons for discussion, not approved production deliverables or a fixed visual system.

The desired balance is **personal memory with present-tense experimentation; a recognisable home with many authors; intimacy with openness; and warmth with playful strangeness**.

## Additional highlighted reading leads

These names and works were marked in the photographs and may support later research. They are not additional sources consulted for this brief.

- **Memory and the counter-history of culture:** Greil Marcus, *Lipstick Traces: A Secret History of the Twentieth Century*; Richard Huelsenbeck's memoirs; Hugo Ball's diary; Ingrid Pfeiffer's critique of bohemian clichés.
- **Urban life and performance:** Peter Jelavich on Berlin cabaret and metropolitan perception; Curt Moreck's guide to Berlin; Aaron Douglas and the Savoy Ballroom; Grete Wiesenthal, Marya Delvard and Mela Mars.
- **Print and collective authorship:** *L'Hydropathe*, *Le Chat noir*, *Schall und Rauch*, *Black Orpheus*, *Actual*, *Irradiador*, *Horizonte*; the Mbari clubs.
- **Women and overlooked creative labour:** Louise France and Marie Krysinska; Loïe Fuller; Frida Strindberg; Jeanne Mammen; Valeska Gert and Anita Berber.
- **Movement and environment:** Giacomo Balla and Fortunato Depero, *Futurist Reconstruction of the Universe*; Balla's movement drawings; Theo van Doesburg and L'Aubette; Piet Mondrian's *Jazz and Neoplasticism*.
- **Critical cautions:** Laura Winkiel on racialised spectacle; Laurence Senelick on popularisation; the book's accounts of colonial imagery, class privilege, exclusion, and political suppression.
